Which multiplication chart to print

  • 1 to 10 is the chart that stops at 100: ten factors across, ten factors down, and no product bigger than ten tens. It is the fastest version to read and the one a child meets first.
  • 1 to 12 adds the eleven and twelve tables that turn up in the times table check, and it is the chart most people mean when they search for a printable multiplication chart. Twelve columns still sit comfortably on a portrait Letter or A4 sheet.
  • 1 to 15 and 1 to 20 are poster grids for a wall or a folder divider. Print them in landscape, or scale the PNG up at a print shop, because twenty columns of digits on one portrait sheet is close to the limit of what stays readable.
  • Every range uses the same grid logic, so a 1 to 20 chart reads exactly like the 1 to 10 chart and a child only has to learn one layout.

Chart, times table or blank practice sheet

  • A multiplication chart is a lookup table: find the row for one factor, the column for the other, and read the product where they cross.
  • The times tables setting writes the same facts out in full - 4 x 1 = 4 through to 4 x 12 = 48 - which is the version to read aloud when a child is learning one table at a time.
  • Half printed keeps every answer above the diagonal and leaves the mirror image below empty, so one sheet covers both looking up and practising.
  • Blank clears every product and leaves the factors and the ruled grid, which makes a fill-in chart for a timed warm up or a laminated sheet that gets wiped clean.
  • The shaded diagonal of square numbers gives a child a landmark: the middle of the grid is always the point where the two factors match.

Printing and reusing the chart

  • Plain 80 to 100 gsm copy paper is right for a worksheet that gets written on. Step up to 160 to 200 gsm card stock, or laminate it, for a chart that lives on a desk and is handled every day.
  • Black and white prints cleanly on a photocopier, which is what a class set needs; the colour kits are for the single sheet that goes on a bedroom wall or inside a binder cover.
  • Landscape is worth choosing for the 1 to 15 and 1 to 20 grids: it turns a cramped portrait table into a poster with cells that are still comfortable to read.

What is the difference between a multiplication chart and a times table?

A multiplication chart is one grid: the factors run across the top and down the side, and the product sits where the row and the column meet, so 7 x 8 reads as 56 in a single glance. A times table is the same facts written out as a list - 7 x 1 = 7, 7 x 2 = 14, 7 x 3 = 21 - which is easier to read aloud when a child is learning one table at a time. This maker prints either version from the same settings.

The 1 to 10 chart covers every product up to 100 and is the version a child meets first. The 1 to 15 and 1 to 20 charts are poster grids: print them in landscape, or scale the PNG up at a print shop for a wall chart, because twenty columns of digits on one portrait sheet is close to the limit of what stays readable.

Can I print a blank multiplication chart?

Yes. Set the answers to blank and the grid prints with the factors across the top and down the side and every product square empty, ready to fill in by hand. For a gentler start choose half printed: the answers above the diagonal stay on the sheet and the rest are left for the child to complete, so the same grid can be attempted twice before the blank copy comes out.

What are the square numbers on a multiplication chart?

They are the products where both factors are the same - 1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36, 49, 64, 81, 100 and so on - and they run diagonally from the top left of the grid to the bottom right. Switch the square number shading on and that diagonal is tinted, which makes the mirror easy to see: 6 x 7 and 7 x 6 both land on 42, so everything above the diagonal repeats below it.

Should I print a multiplication chart in colour or black and white?

Colour helps a chart that goes on a desk or a wall, because the heading band and the shaded diagonal pull the eye to the right row and column, and the eight colour kits run from rainbow down to a plain black and white version. Choose black and white for photocopying a class set, for a child who finds a busy sheet distracting, or when you would rather save the colour ink.
